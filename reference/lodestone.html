Cognitive architecture · no neural network

Lodestone

A mind made of a few hundred numbers. It starts knowing nothing, works out what feeds it and what hurts it, sets its own goals, and notices when the world's rules change underneath it.

    How it works

    One cycle per step. Nothing is trained in advance, and there are no weights, layers or gradients. The agent's only data is what happens to its own body.

    1. Feel

      Two body levels, energy and integrity, are turned into distress: zero when full, rising steeply near empty. Distress is the only thing the agent cares about. It is never given a score or a goal.

    2. Remember in two speeds

      Every belief (what a colour, a shape, or an exact kind does to each level) keeps a slow average and its latest experience. When the two disagree by more than that belief's usual scatter, the belief is in dissent and the latest experience is trusted.

    3. Fold

      Two dissents in a row and the belief folds: the old average is thrown away. If it was a long-held rule, that counts as an upheaval. Everything learned before it is demoted to rumour and becomes worth re-testing.

    4. Predict by vote

      For any object, the exact kind, its colour and its shape each vote, weighted by how consistent their history is. A feature that has never made a difference abstains. An untried kind is only as well known as its least-known feature.

    5. Be pulled

      Every remembered place pulls on the agent: objects by the distress they are expected to remove plus what could be learned, unseen and long-unvisited ground by how urgent things are. Distance weakens the pull. The strongest pull becomes the goal.

    6. Stay alive while learning

      It learns which needs get worse by waiting (hunger) and which recover (injury). It will not experiment when the worst thing it has ever felt could finish off a need that would heal on its own.

    The experiment

    Forty generated worlds, 3,000 steps each. Halfway through, the hidden rules are reshuffled without warning, so the best food and the dangerous shape both change. Five agents live in the same forty worlds. This runs in your browser when the page loads; none of these worlds were used while the design was being tuned.

    How it differs, and what it is for

    Compared with other approaches

    • Neural networks learn from large datasets by adjusting many weights. Lodestone has none; it learns a rule from one or two experiences and you can read every belief it holds.
    • Reinforcement learners maximise a reward signal someone defined. Lodestone has no reward, value table or policy. Behaviour falls out of body distress and doubt.
    • Classic symbolic architectures such as Soar and ACT-R run on hand-written production rules. Lodestone is given no rules about its world.
    • Most learners treat change as noise to average away. Here disagreement between old and new memory is the central signal: it sets what to trust, what to doubt and what to re-test.

    Where a design like this fits

    • Small devices and robots with a battery, wear limits and no room for a model: learn which actions drain or damage them and adapt when conditions change.
    • Game characters and simulated creatures that need believable, self-motivated behaviour at almost no cost.
    • Monitoring agents that must notice that a familiar pattern has stopped holding, and say which belief broke.
    • Teaching and research: a complete agent small enough to read in one sitting.

    Footprint

    • The whole mind and world: about 13 KB of plain JavaScript, no libraries.
    • Learned state: a few hundred numbers.
    • One decision costs one pass over the map. A full 3,000-step life runs in a fraction of a second on a laptop.

    Limits, stated plainly

    • It has been tested only in this grid world. It does not handle language, images or long-range planning.
    • It does not always survive. Some worlds starve it before it finds food, and a rule change can hurt it three times before it has re-learned enough.
    • The design is original as a whole, but its parts have relatives: drive-based agents, weighting cues by reliability, change detection. Its building blocks are ordinary ones: running averages, a shortest-path search, a logarithm.
